The Problem

Children struggle to stay engaged with traditional language learning tools

Existing digital learning platforms often fail young learners — they're either too complex, visually dull, or lack the motivation systems children need to build consistent study habits.

We were tasked with designing a solution that felt genuinely fun, not just educational with a bright color scheme slapped on top.

🎮

Engagement gap

Children abandon learning apps within days without game-like reward loops

📱

Multi-device reality

Kids switch between phone, tablet, desktop — the experience needed to work everywhere

🏆

Motivation matters

Without visible progress and rewards, young learners lose the drive to return daily

Our Process

From research to three platforms

We followed a structured design process grounded in Nielsen's heuristics, moving from user research through to high-fidelity prototypes across all three platforms.

01 — Research

Understand the user

Studied children's learning patterns and identified that motivation systems and visual feedback are critical for this age group.

02 — Define

Set objectives

Defined core features: flashcards, interactive quizzes, streak tracking, avatar rewards, and multi-platform access.

03 — Design

Build & iterate

Wireframed all screens in Figma, applied color psychology and visual hierarchy, then prototyped the full flows.

04 — Evaluate

Heuristic review

Evaluated each screen against Nielsen's 10 heuristics and Shneiderman's golden rules, documenting decisions.

The Design

Three platforms, one cohesive experience

Smart Kids was designed for web, mobile, and Apple Vision Pro — each adapting the same core learning experience to the affordances of that device.

The mobile app adapts the website's content into a thumb-friendly vertical flow. A universal top navigation bar (back arrow + hamburger) appears on every screen. When inside a game, it transforms into a context bar showing the game name and the user's streak/diamond count — always visible, never intrusive.

The Apple Vision Pro interface was the most innovative challenge. Since typing is awkward in VR, we designed a QR scan login — the user double-blinks to open the camera and scans their code from a phone or tablet. No keyboard needed.

The VR home screen shows the avatar standing beside the user in their real space, browsable with gaze and tap. Games open in a wide three-panel layout — sidebar navigation on the left, game content in the centre, avatar on the right.

Key Design Decisions

Why we made these choices

Every design decision was documented and justified against a recognised UI/UX principle. Here are the most impactful ones.

Color

Gradient background: #F8FFFA → #9BE5B4

Green-to-white gradients are associated with nature, growth, and calm — perfect for a children's learning environment. This palette reduces eye fatigue during extended sessions and signals a positive, encouraging space. Aligned with Nielsen's Aesthetic and Minimalist Design.

Navigation

Active tab changes background color, not just icon

Young users rely on strong visual cues. Rather than a subtle underline, the active navigation button shifts its entire background color — making the current section unmistakable at a glance. This directly applies Visibility of System Status.

Gamification

Streaks + diamonds + avatar shop reward system

Children are motivated by visible progress and tangible rewards. The streak counter (daily learning) combined with diamonds (earned from quizzes) spent in an avatar shop creates a virtuous loop — learning earns currency, currency enables personalisation, personalisation deepens identity investment in the app.

Accessibility

Sidebar navigation + arrow indicators in game screens

Rather than requiring users to remember what categories exist, we kept the sidebar visible and added directional arrows to indicate scrollable content. Children especially benefit from Recognition over Recall — they should never have to remember, only choose.

VR Input

QR code login instead of virtual keyboard

Typing in VR is one of the biggest friction points for new users. By allowing users to scan a QR code from their phone via a double-blink gesture, we removed the biggest login barrier on this platform entirely — reducing effort from ~30 keystrokes to 1 gesture.

Heuristic Evaluation

Designing against Nielsen's 10 heuristics

Every major screen was evaluated against Nielsen's framework during the design process, not just at the end.

Visibility of system status

Active nav states, in-game progress bars, streak counters, and verification step indicators all keep users informed of where they are and what's happened.

User control and freedom

Multiple login options, password reset, game pause/restart/exit at any time, and a guest mode ensure users are never trapped or forced down a single path.

Consistency and standards

Same color scheme, layout patterns, and navigation across all pages. Green = correct, red = incorrect — universal conventions respected throughout.

Recognition over recall

Sidebar navigation keeps all categories visible. Arrow indicators show scrollable content. Icons are labelled. Children are never expected to remember instructions.

Aesthetic and minimalist design

Each screen communicates one primary action. Content is grouped clearly, with generous whitespace that doesn't overwhelm young users visually.

Error prevention

Email verification with resend option, in-game settings pause (not quit) by default, and clear cancel buttons throughout prevent accidental data loss.

Reflection

What I learned and what I'd do next

What I learned

  • Designing for children requires more deliberate visual hierarchy than adult interfaces — you can't rely on text alone
  • Applying heuristics during design (not just evaluation) leads to better decisions earlier and fewer revisions
  • Designing for VR forced me to rethink fundamental assumptions about input — gestures replace every tap and click
  • Consistent design tokens (colours, spacing, typography) across platforms made the project manageable as a team

What I'd do next

  • Run usability testing with actual children aged 6–12 to validate whether the navigation is as intuitive as assumed
  • Explore accessibility features: audio descriptions, high-contrast mode, and adjustable font size
  • Design the onboarding flow for parents — parental controls and progress reporting were out of scope but are critical
  • Prototype the reward shop in higher fidelity and test whether the diamond economy motivates the right behaviours
